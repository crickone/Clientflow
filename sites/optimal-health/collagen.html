The scaffold everything else hangs on.

Collagen is the body's scaffold. It is the protein that keeps skin firm and elastic, and the one that gives tendons and ligaments their strength. Most of what you think of as holding you together is made of it.

You lay down new collagen all your life, and you break old collagen down all your life. What changes is the balance between the two. Age, sun, training load, hormones and injury all slow the making and speed the breaking, and the result arrives quietly rather than overnight: skin with less spring in it than it had, connective tissue that grumbles under load it used to take without comment.

Nothing on this page stops that arithmetic. What a run of sessions is understood to do is lean on it a little, in the direction of making rather than breaking, and slowly enough that the honest unit is a month rather than an evening.

What a fibroblast does with light.

Our bed emits red and near-infrared light at five wavelengths: 633, 660, 810, 850 and 940 nm. Those numbers are a specification of the machine, in the way that a kettle has a wattage, and they are the same five whether you booked the bed for your skin or for your hamstrings. Red is the part you can see. Near-infrared is the part you cannot, and it is the part that carries furthest into tissue.

The process has a name — photobiomodulation — and the mechanism, as far as it is understood, runs like this. Light at those wavelengths passes into skin and the connective tissue beneath it much as sunlight passes through a window. Inside the cell it is absorbed by the mitochondria, the part that makes the cell's energy, and the working account is that they can then make more of it.

Which cells is the whole of the interest on this page. Fibroblasts are the ones that make collagen, and with more energy to hand they can lay down more procollagen, the raw form the body assembles the finished material from. The enzymes that take collagen apart are thought to dial back over the same stretch, so the balance can shift a little towards building. Microcirculation can improve alongside both, which matters for the plain reason that it is how the material to build with gets there at all.

Every verb in that paragraph is a “can” rather than a “will”, and we would rather write the hedge down than let the grammar smuggle it. It is what the process is understood to encourage across a run of sessions, not what one twelve-minute session hands you. That is also why the stages further down are counted in weeks and not in visits.

What this is not

We do not diagnose anything, we do not offer clinical skin work, and we do not take before-and-after pictures — two photographs taken in two different lights settle nothing, and we would rather not ask you to judge yourself against one. What people use it for.

Two things, and the shortness of the list is the point. Red and near-infrared light gets offered in other rooms for work that sits on the clinical side of a line this clinic does not cross, and rather than borrow that language we have left it out. What follows is what people here actually book the bed for.

Skin firmness and texture

  • Supports the skin's own collagen production over a run of sessions
  • Encourages circulation to the skin, which is what carries oxygen and nutrients to it
  • May support elasticity, so skin feels springier rather than slack
  • May support the skin's own renewal and its barrier, which is most of what people mean by looking well

Tendon, ligament and joint comfort

  • Acts on the connective tissue that carries load — tendon, ligament and fascia
  • May encourage collagen to organise, which is what load tolerance is built out of
  • Supports joint comfort and easy range of movement
  • May ease tightness around the joints and the stiffness that follows a hard session or a long week at a desk
  • Used alongside whatever your physio or your coach has you doing, never instead of it

The infrared page

Twelve weeks, and what each part of them is for.

This is not a forecast of how you will look. It is an account of what the process is understood to be doing at each stage, written down so you have something honest to measure the rhythm against.

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Circulation, not collagen.

    In the first fortnight the light is thought to act on cellular energy and on microcirculation: blood flow to the area being warmed can increase, and the inflammatory signalling around it can begin to settle. Skin often looks better hydrated, tissue tends to feel calmer, and people commonly say they look a little fresher. It is worth being plain about what that is and is not. No collagen has been rebuilt at this stage. Anything you notice in the first two weeks is blood flow, lymphatic drainage and a quieter inflammatory picture — the groundwork, not the build.

  2. Weeks 4–6

    The fibroblasts start building.

    This is the window in which the collagen-making cells can begin laying down new procollagen, along with the molecules that hold water in the dermis. Skin can feel springier and hold moisture better; in tendon and ligament, early collagen organisation is thought to begin around here. Any early change in firmness belongs to this stage rather than the last one. Consistency carries it, and so does eating enough protein and vitamin C, which is what the body builds collagen out of.

  3. Weeks 8–12+

    Reorganising, not just adding.

    Later on, the working account is that the fibres reorganise and align rather than simply accumulate, with more of the mature Type I collagen forming and the enzymes that break collagen down dialling back. Connective tissue is thought to sit more comfortably under gradually increasing load around here. This is the slow half of the work, and the half a fortnight cannot reach. On a twelve-week timescale the useful question is upkeep, not how fast anything can be built.
