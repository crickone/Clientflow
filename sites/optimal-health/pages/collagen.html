The scaffold everything else hangs on.

Collagen is the body's scaffold: the protein that keeps skin firm and elastic, and the one that gives tendons and ligaments their strength. Most of what you think of as holding you together is made of it.

You lay down new collagen all your life, and you break old collagen down all your life. Over the years the balance between them tips. Age, sun, training load, hormones and injury all slow the making and speed the breaking, and the result comes on quietly: skin with less spring in it than it had, connective tissue that grumbles under load it used to take without comment.

Nothing on this page stops that. A run of sessions is understood to lean on that balance a little, towards making rather than breaking, and slowly enough that the honest unit is a month, not an evening.

What a fibroblast does with light.

Our bed emits red and near-infrared light at five wavelengths: 633, 660, 810, 850 and 940 nm. Those are a specification of the machine, the way a kettle has a wattage, and they're the same five whether you booked the bed for your skin or for your hamstrings. Red is the part you can see. Near-infrared is the part you can't, and it carries furthest into tissue.

The process has a name — photobiomodulation — and as far as it's understood, the mechanism runs like this. Light at those wavelengths passes into skin and the connective tissue beneath it much as sunlight passes through a window. Inside the cell the mitochondria absorb it — they're the part that makes the cell's energy — and the working account is that they can then make more of it.

Which cells take it in is the part that matters here. Fibroblasts are the ones that make collagen, and with more energy to hand they can lay down more procollagen, the raw form the body builds the finished material from. The enzymes that take collagen apart are thought to dial back over the same stretch, so the balance can shift a little towards building. Microcirculation can improve alongside both, which matters because that's how the material to build with gets there at all.

Every verb in that paragraph is a “can” rather than a “will”, and we'd rather write that down than hope you spot it. This is what the process is understood to encourage across a run of sessions, not what one twelve-minute session hands you. That's also why the stages further down are counted in weeks rather than visits.

One practical thing falls out of the mechanism. The light has to land on skin to get in, and clothing blocks it, so you undress for the bed. The less you have on, the more of your skin the twelve minutes reaches, and with nothing on all of it gets the light. Underwear is fine and plenty of people keep it on. You're on your own in the room, and it's your call rather than ours.

The infrared page

Twelve weeks, and what each part of them is for.

This isn't a forecast of how you'll look. It's an account of what the process is understood to be doing at each stage, written down so you've got something honest to measure the rhythm against.

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Circulation, not collagen.

    In the first fortnight the light is thought to act on cellular energy and on microcirculation. Blood flow to the area being warmed can increase, and the inflammatory signalling around it can begin to settle. Skin often looks better hydrated, tissue tends to feel calmer, and people commonly say they look a little fresher. It's worth being plain about what that is and isn't. No collagen has been rebuilt at this stage. Anything you notice in the first two weeks is blood flow, lymphatic drainage and a quieter inflammatory picture — the groundwork, not the build.

  2. Weeks 4–6

    The fibroblasts start building.

    This is the window where the collagen-making cells can begin laying down new procollagen, along with the molecules that hold water in the dermis. Skin can feel springier and hold moisture better, and in tendon and ligament, early collagen organisation is thought to begin around here. Any early change in firmness belongs to this stage, not the last one. Consistency carries it, and so does eating enough protein and vitamin C, because that's what the body builds collagen out of.

  3. Weeks 8–12+

    Reorganising, not just adding.

    Later on, the working account is that the fibres reorganise and align, not simply accumulate, with more of the mature Type I collagen forming and the enzymes that break collagen down dialling back. Connective tissue is thought to sit more comfortably under gradually increasing load around here. This is the slow half of the work, and the half a fortnight can't reach. Over twelve weeks the useful question is upkeep, not how fast anything can be built.
