Built for the week around the training.

Runners, rugby and GAA players, cyclists, lifters, Hyrox athletes and fighters — and a fair number of people who train five mornings a week and would never call themselves athletes. What they have in common is a week with more in it than one rest day can absorb.

None of what is in this building is training. It will not make you fitter, and nothing in the room adds a session to your week. What it is for is the space around the training: the evening after a heavy lift, the day between a match and the next one, the week after a race when everything still feels flat.

You do not need to know which therapy you want before you arrive. Tell us what your week looks like and what it is doing to you, and we will tell you which of the four is worth your hour — and which one is not.

Four therapies, and what each is actually for.

Each has a page of its own with the full account of how it works, how long it takes and what it does not do. What follows is the short version, in the only terms that matter on a Tuesday evening: what it asks of you, and where it goes in a week.

One thing out loud, once, rather than left to the verbs. Everything below is written as “may” and “supports” because that is what the evidence carries. These are accounts of what each therapy is understood to do, not forecasts of what it will do for you, and not one of them is a substitute for training, sleep or food. If you want a figure for what any of it will do to your times, we do not have one.

Hyperbaric oxygen

45–60 minutes · you sit still

The one both athletes further down this page are talking about. You sit in a pressurised chamber and breathe air with more oxygen in it than the room has; under pressure some of that oxygen dissolves into the plasma rather than waiting on red blood cells, so the blood can carry more of it than usual. Most people book it the day after a match, a race or a long run, and through the heavy weeks. It asks nothing of you but the hour.

Infrared

12 minutes · you lie down

Twelve minutes lying fully clothed under red and near-infrared light, which cells take in through the mitochondria. This is the easiest thing here to fit around a session: no load, nothing to recover from, and it sits fine on a rest day or between two sessions on the same day. People book it for next-day stiffness and for the evening after a late finish, which is also the reason a fair few fall asleep on it.

See infrared

The HIFEM chair

20–30 minutes · muscle work

You sit fully clothed and a focused magnetic field prompts the muscle underneath to contract. Worth being straight about, because it is the one people get wrong: this is muscle work, not a rest-day therapy. Plan it the way you would plan a gym session rather than assuming it is free — not stacked on top of a hard one, and not the day before something that matters until you know how you respond to it.

See HIFEM

Massage

30–60 minutes · hands-on

Therapeutic massage, reflexology and lymphatic drainage, from clinical therapists rather than from a machine. This is the one to book when something in particular has been tight for a fortnight and you want somebody to put their hands on it and tell you what they find. Depth and length are set with you; say so if you want it working rather than relaxing, or the other way round.

Where it goes in the week.

Five occasions people actually use us for, in the order they tend to come up. If your week does not look like any of them, ring us and describe it.

  1. Post-session

    The same evening, or the day after.

    This is what most people book, and it is the simplest use of the place. Infrared in the evening after a heavy lift or a long run; the chamber the day after a match or a race. Neither adds anything to the week's load, which is the point — you are not training, you are sitting still while something else happens. What people tell us they notice first is next-day stiffness and how heavy the legs feel going into the next session. We would not promise you that, and one visit is not the test of it either; a run of them across a block is.

  2. Pre-event

    Not the week to try something new.

    If you want the chamber or the bed in the days before a race or a match, have it in your legs already. A session you have done a dozen times is predictable and one you have never done is not, and the week of an event is the worst possible week to find out which you are. The chair especially: it is muscle work, so it belongs in the taper the same way any other session does. Plenty of people book the bed the evening before and find it settles them. Plenty leave everything alone. Do what you already know about yourself.

  3. Niggles

    Alongside somebody else's plan, not instead of one.

    We are a recovery room. We do not assess injuries, we do not put a rehabilitation plan together, and nothing here is a substitute for the person who is actually looking after it. If you are working with a physio or a doctor, their plan is the plan, and this is something you can put around it with their say-so. Bring us what you have been told to avoid and we will work inside it — and if what you describe sounds like it belongs with a clinician rather than with us, we will tell you so and you can keep your hour.

  4. Heavy blocks

    Little and often, through the loaded weeks.

    Through a heavy block or a run of fixtures, short sessions at a steady rhythm suit the schedule better than one long visit booked after things have already come apart. Two or three infrared sessions a week is a common pattern; the chamber once or twice. The honest case for it is consistency rather than rescue, and it is the same case as for sleep: easier to stay on top of a block than to climb back into one.

  5. Travel days

    After the bus, and between the rounds.

    Long drives and flights leave people stiff and flat, and a multi-day competition leaves very little room between rounds to do anything about it. Infrared is the easiest thing to drop into a gap: twelve minutes, clothes on, nothing to recover from afterwards. The chamber wants a longer window, so it tends to suit the evening before or the morning after rather than the middle of a day of heats. If you are passing through Clonmel around a fixture, ring ahead and we will find you a slot.

What athletes ask us first.

Will this make me faster?

No, and we would rather say so on the way in than have you work it out on the way home. Training makes you faster. Sleep and food decide how much of the training you get to keep. What a recovery room can support is the hours in between, and the only honest measure of it is your own: whether a block goes better than the last one did, and whether you arrive at the hard sessions in better shape than you usually do.

Does a session take anything out of me?

Infrared, hyperbaric oxygen and massage do not. You lie or sit still, there is nothing to recover from, and any of the three sits fine on a rest day or between two sessions on the same day.

The chair is the exception, and it is worth repeating: it is muscle work. Plan it like a gym session rather than assuming it is free, and if you have never sat in it, do not put the first one the day before something that matters.

When in the week should I book?

If you are only coming once, come after the hardest thing you do — that is the hour you are most likely to notice. Past that, most people settle into the bed in the evening for wind-down and the chamber in the morning for a clear head, and place the chair and a massage where the week has room for them.

I am coming back from an injury. Can I use this?

Talk to whoever is looking after it first, and bring us what they said. We are a recovery room rather than a physiotherapy or medical service: we do not assess injuries and we do not build a rehabilitation plan. What we can do is work inside somebody else's, and tell you honestly if what you are describing sounds like it belongs with a physio or a GP rather than with us.

I get tested. Is any of this a problem?

Nothing here is a substance and nothing is administered to you — it is oxygen under pressure, light, a magnetic field and a pair of hands. That said, the rules belong to your governing body and not to us, and we are not going to tell you what your sport does and does not permit. If you are in a testing pool, check with your federation or your national anti-doping body before you book, and we will give you whatever detail about the equipment they ask for.

Do I have to buy a block?

You do not have to, and a block is still what we would recommend. One session tells you whether you get on with it, which is a fair thing to settle before you spend more. It is not the test of any of this: as the page above says, the case for a recovery room is a run of sessions across a block rather than a visit after a bad week. That is what a block is for, and it brings the per-session price down as well.

Come once first if you would rather decide for yourself than take a website's word for it. If you already know you want it in the week, buy the block.
