Built for the week around the training.

Runners, rugby and GAA players, cyclists, lifters, Hyrox athletes and fighters — and a fair number of people who train five mornings a week and would never call themselves athletes. They all have a week with more in it than one rest day can absorb.

Nothing in this building is training. It won't make you fitter, and nothing in the room adds a session to your week. It's for the space around your training: the evening after a heavy lift, the day between a match and the next one, the week after a race when everything still feels flat.

You don't need to know which therapy you want before you arrive. Tell us what your week looks like and what it's doing to you, and we'll tell you which of the four is worth your hour — and which one isn't.

Four therapies, and what each is actually for.

Each has a page of its own with the full account of how it works, how long it takes and what it doesn't do. Here's the short version: what it asks of you, and where it goes in a week.

Everything below is written as “may” and “supports” because that's as far as the evidence goes. These are accounts of what each therapy is understood to do, not forecasts of what it will do for you, and not one of them is a substitute for training, sleep or food. If you want a figure for what any of it will do to your times, we don't have one.

Hyperbaric oxygen

45–60 minutes · you sit still

The one athletes here book most. You sit in a pressurised chamber and breathe air with more oxygen in it than the room has; under pressure some of that oxygen dissolves into the plasma rather than waiting on red blood cells, so the blood can carry more of it than usual. Most people book it the day after a match, a race or a long run, and through the heavy weeks. It asks nothing of you but the hour.

See hyperbaric oxygen

Infrared

12 minutes · you lie down

Twelve minutes lying under red and near-infrared light, which cells take in through the mitochondria. You undress for it: the light has to land on skin to get in, so the less you have on the more of you it reaches. This is the easiest thing here to fit around a session: no load, nothing to recover from, and it sits fine on a rest day or between two sessions on the same day. People book it for next-day stiffness and for the evening after a late finish, which is also why a fair few fall asleep on it.

See infrared

The HIFEM chair

20–30 minutes · muscle work

You sit fully clothed and a focused magnetic field prompts the muscle underneath to contract. This is the one people get wrong. It's muscle work, not a rest-day therapy. Plan it the way you'd plan a gym session. Not stacked on top of a hard one, and not the day before something that matters until you know how you respond to it.

See HIFEM

Massage

30–60 minutes · hands-on

Therapeutic massage, reflexology and lymphatic drainage, from clinical therapists, not from a machine. This is the one to book when something in particular has been tight for a fortnight and you want somebody to put their hands on it and tell you what they find. We set depth and length with you, so say which you want: working, or relaxing.

Where it goes in the week.

Five occasions people actually use us for, in the order they tend to come up. If your week doesn't look like any of them, ring us and describe it.

  1. Post-session

    The same evening, or the day after.

    This is what most people book, and it's the simplest use of the place. Infrared in the evening after a heavy lift or a long run; the chamber the day after a match or a race. Neither adds anything to the week's load. You're not training, you're sitting still while something else happens. People tell us the first thing they notice is next-day stiffness and how heavy the legs feel going into the next session. We wouldn't promise you that, and one visit isn't the test of it either — a run of them across a block is.

  2. Pre-event

    Not the week to try something new.

    If you want the chamber or the bed in the days before a race or a match, have it in your legs already. A session you've done a dozen times is predictable; one you've never done isn't, and the week of an event is the worst week to find that out. That goes double for the chair. It's muscle work, so it belongs in the taper the same way any other session does. Plenty of people book the bed the evening before and find it settles them. Plenty leave everything alone. Go with what you already know about yourself.

  3. Niggles

    Alongside somebody else's plan, not instead of one.

    We're a recovery room. We don't assess injuries, we don't put a rehabilitation plan together, and nothing here is a substitute for the person actually looking after it. If you're working with a physio or a doctor, their plan is the plan, and this is something you can put around it with their say-so. Bring us what you've been told to avoid and we'll work inside it. If what you describe sounds like it belongs with a clinician rather than with us, we'll say so and you can keep your hour.

  4. Heavy blocks

    Little and often, through the loaded weeks.

    Through a heavy block or a run of fixtures, short sessions at a steady rhythm suit the schedule better than one long visit booked after things have already come apart. Two or three infrared sessions a week is a common pattern; the chamber once or twice. The honest case for it is consistency, not rescue — the same case as for sleep. It's easier to stay on top of a block than to climb back into one.

  5. Travel days

    After the bus, and between the rounds.

    Long drives and flights leave people stiff and flat, and a multi-day competition leaves very little room between rounds to do anything about it. Infrared is the easiest thing to drop into a gap: twelve minutes, and nothing to recover from afterwards. You undress for it, so allow a few minutes either side rather than none. The chamber wants a longer window, so it tends to suit the evening before or the morning after rather than the middle of a day of heats. If you're passing through Clonmel around a fixture, ring ahead and we'll find you a slot.

What athletes ask us first.

Will this make me faster?

No, and we'd rather say so on the way in than have you work it out on the way home. Training makes you faster. Sleep and food decide how much of the training you get to keep. A recovery room can support the hours in between, and the only honest measure of it is your own: whether a block goes better than the last one did, and whether you arrive at the hard sessions in better shape than you usually do.

Does a session take anything out of me?

Infrared, hyperbaric oxygen and massage don't. You lie or sit still, there's nothing to recover from, and any of the three sits fine on a rest day or between two sessions on the same day.

The chair is the exception. It's muscle work, so plan it like a gym session, and if you've never sat in it, don't put the first one the day before something that matters.

When in the week should I book?

If you're only coming once, come after the hardest thing you do, because that's the hour you're most likely to notice. After that, most people settle into the bed in the evening for wind-down and the chamber in the morning for a clear head, and put the chair and a massage where the week has room for them.

Tell us what your week actually looks like when you ring and we'll space them sensibly rather than selling you all four.

I'm coming back from an injury. Can I use this?

Talk to whoever is looking after it first, and bring us what they said. We're a recovery room, not a physiotherapy or medical service. We don't assess injuries and we don't build a rehabilitation plan. We can work inside somebody else's plan, and tell you honestly if what you're describing sounds like it belongs with a physio or a GP rather than with us.

I get tested. Is any of this a problem?

Nothing here is a substance and nothing is administered to you. It's oxygen under pressure, light, a magnetic field and a pair of hands. That said, the rules belong to your governing body, not to us, and we're not going to tell you what your sport does and doesn't permit. If you're in a testing pool, check with your federation or your national anti-doping body before you book, and we'll give you whatever detail about the equipment they ask for.

Do I have to buy a block?

You don't have to, and we'd still recommend one. One session tells you whether you get on with it, which is a fair thing to settle before you spend more. It isn't the test of any of this, though. The case for a recovery room is a run of sessions across a block rather than a visit after a bad week. That's what a block is for, and it brings the per-session price down as well.

Come once first if you'd rather decide for yourself than take a website's word for it. If you already know you want it in the week, buy the block.
