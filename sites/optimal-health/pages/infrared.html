Light you feel as warmth.

The bed gives off red and near-infrared light rather than hot air. That light passes into skin and the connective tissue under it, which is why the warmth reads as deep and even rather than stifling — nearer to standing in spring sunshine than to sitting in a sauna.

You lie down in comfortable clothes, the bed warms gradually, and the whole visit is over inside half an hour. There is no sweating to speak of, no effort, and nothing to recover from afterwards. Most people book it for the evening, on the grounds that it is easier to wind down after than to work after.

Energy the cell can spend.

Our bed emits red and near-infrared light at five wavelengths: 633, 660, 810, 850 and 940 nm. Those numbers are a specification of the machine, in the way that a kettle has a wattage. Red is the part of it you can see. Near-infrared is the part you cannot, and it is the part that carries furthest into tissue.

The mechanism, as far as it is understood, runs like this. Light at those wavelengths passes into skin and connective tissue much as sunlight passes through a window, and cells absorb it the way a solar panel absorbs daylight. What absorbs it inside the cell is the mitochondria, the part that makes the cell's energy, and the working account is that they can make more usable energy when they do.

With more of that energy to hand, a cell can do more of its ordinary work. Fibroblasts — the cells that make collagen — can lay down more of it. Small blood vessels can open a little wider, so more oxygen and nutrients reach the area being warmed. The signalling that keeps tissue irritated can settle. Every one of those is a “can” rather than a “will”: it is what the process is understood to encourage over a run of sessions, not what one session delivers.

Which is why the account further down this page is written in weeks.

What the first twelve weeks are doing.

This is not a promise about how you will look or feel. It is an account of what the process is understood to be doing at each stage, set out so you can judge the rhythm against something honest rather than against a photograph.

Weeks 1–2

Circulation, not collagen.

In the first fortnight the light is working on cellular energy and on microcirculation: more blood to the area being warmed, and the inflammatory signalling around it beginning to settle. Tissue often feels calmer and less tight, and people commonly say they look a little fresher. It is worth being plain about what that is. No collagen has been rebuilt at this stage. Whatever you notice in the first two weeks is blood flow, lymphatic drainage and a quieter inflammatory picture — the groundwork, not the build.

Weeks 4–6

Fibroblasts get to work.

This is the window in which the collagen-making cells can begin laying down new procollagen, along with the molecules that hold water in the dermis. Skin can feel springier and hold moisture better; in tendon and ligament, early collagen organisation is thought to begin around here. Any early change in firmness belongs to this stage rather than the last one. Consistency is what carries it, and so does eating enough protein and vitamin C, which is what the body builds collagen out of.

Weeks 8–12+

Reorganising, not just adding.

Later on the fibres start to reorganise and align rather than simply accumulate, with more of the mature Type I collagen forming and the enzymes that break collagen down dialling back. This is the slow half of the work, and the half a fortnight cannot reach. It is also why people who get this far tend to space sessions out rather than stop: on a twelve-week timescale the useful question is upkeep, not how fast anything can be built.

What people ask before they book.

What does it feel like?

Deep, even warmth that should feel soothing rather than hot. You lie back fully clothed and get comfortably warm, and most people get off the bed loose and calm. A fair number fall asleep. If it ever feels too warm, say so — we can turn it down or stop.

Is it a sunbed?

No. A sunbed works by emitting ultraviolet light, and this bed emits none. It runs in the red and near-infrared bands — 633, 660, 810, 850 and 940 nm — which sit at the far side of visible light from ultraviolet. There is no tanning and no burning, and nothing about a session is meant to change the colour of your skin.

Is this a medical treatment?

No. What we run is wellness-focused infrared warmth for relaxation, comfort and rest. We do not diagnose medical conditions and we do not claim to resolve them. If you have a diagnosed condition, follow your clinician's advice, and tell us about it before you book so we can say honestly whether this suits you.

How long is a session, and how often should I come?

The bed itself runs fifteen to twenty-five minutes, so the whole visit sits inside half an hour. Most people start with two or three a week for a couple of weeks, then settle into whatever fits their evenings and their training.

The stages above are written in weeks rather than in sessions because that is the timescale the series works on. There is nothing wrong with coming once; it is simply a different thing from coming for three months.

What should I wear, and do I need to prepare?

Comfortable everyday clothing, and arrive hydrated. Skip a heavy meal immediately beforehand. Beyond that there is nothing to prepare — we set the bed up, check you are comfortable, and leave you to it.

Will I sweat?

Some people perspire lightly and some do not, and either is normal. This is not a sweat session: the point is comfortable warmth rather than endurance, and we would rather keep you in the relaxing zone than push you out of it. Have a glass of water afterwards either way.

Can I use it on the same day as training?

Yes. Plenty of people train earlier in the day and book the bed in the evening to wind down. It adds no training load of its own, which also makes it useful on a rest day when you want recovery without doing any more work.

A common week is hyperbaric oxygen in the morning for a clear head and infrared in the evening for sleep, with HIFEM or a massage placed where they suit you. Tell us what your week looks like and we will space them sensibly.

Who should check with a GP first?

Anyone pregnant or trying to conceive, anyone with cardiovascular conditions, heat intolerance, a photosensitive condition, or on medication that raises sensitivity to heat or light. If you have skin sensitivities, ask your GP or dermatologist. For teenagers we need guardian consent, and for older adults with health considerations we may ask for GP guidance before going ahead.
