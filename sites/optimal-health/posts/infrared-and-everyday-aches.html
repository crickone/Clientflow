Infrared and everyday aches: what the light actually does

Twelve minutes under red and near-infrared light. What the bed emits, what the light is understood to do once it's in tissue, and what people say afterwards.

All articles · More on infrared

What people come in about

Shoulders stiff from a week at a desk. Hips sore after a long drive. A back that's worse some weeks than others, and morning stiffness that wasn't there a few years ago.

What the bed emits

Red and near-infrared light at five wavelengths: 633, 660, 810, 850 and 940 nm, and no ultraviolet. Red is the light you can see; near-infrared is invisible and reaches deepest into tissue.

It's not a sunbed: there's no ultraviolet, so no tanning and no burning.

What the light is understood to do

Cells absorb the light, mainly in the mitochondria, the part of the cell that makes energy. The current understanding is that they can then make more usable energy.

With more energy, cells can do more of their normal work: small blood vessels can widen to bring in more oxygen and nutrients, collagen-making cells can lay down more collagen, and irritated tissue can settle.

These changes build over a run of sessions.

You undress for the bed, because clothing blocks the light. The less you wear, the more of you it reaches; underwear is fine. You're on your own in the room.

What it feels like

Even warmth that builds gradually. You barely sweat and there's nothing to recover from. If it feels too warm, tell us and we'll turn it down.

What people say afterwards

People say they feel looser through the hips, back and shoulders, that next-morning stiffness is easier, and that they sleep better that night. That's what clients report, not a promise.

What it isn't

It's not a medical treatment, and we don't diagnose or treat anything. If you have a diagnosed condition, follow your doctor's advice and tell us before you book.

The session

The bed runs twelve minutes. The visit sits inside half an hour.
