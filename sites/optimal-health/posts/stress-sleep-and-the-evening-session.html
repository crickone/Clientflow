Stress, sleep, and the twelve minutes after work

The most dependable thing an evening session gives you is half an hour nobody can interrupt. The bed is what makes you keep the appointment.

A week that never quite switches off

Most people who book an evening session are not unwell. They have had a run of weeks with more in them than the weeks were built for: late finishes, a phone that keeps going, sleep that has quietly gone off somewhere around Tuesday. None of that has a name, and none of it needs one before it is worth doing something about.

What the evening session actually is

Twelve minutes on the infrared bed, in whatever you arrived in. The bed gives off red and near-infrared light rather than hot air, so the warmth reads as deep and even rather than stifling — nearer to standing in spring sunshine than to sitting in a sauna. There is little sweating to speak of, no effort, and nothing to recover from afterwards. The whole visit sits inside half an hour.

People book it for the evening on the grounds that it is easier to wind down after than to work after. A fair number fall asleep on it, which is fine.

The part that is not the machine

It is worth saying the unglamorous thing out loud. A good share of what an evening session gives you is a bounded block of time in a quiet room where your phone is not. We would rather you left it alone for the twelve minutes, and most people find that the hardest instruction in the building.

Booked time is easier to keep than good intentions. That is not a claim about light; it is a claim about calendars, and it is the one thing here we are completely sure of.

What people say about the night after

The two things mentioned most often are that the evening wound down more easily than usual, and that they slept better that night. That is what clients tell us rather than a promise we can make, and we would rather write it that way round. Sleep has a great many inputs and a twelve-minute session is one of them.

For a different kind of tired

Where a week has left you slow rather than wound up, people tend to book the chamber instead. That is forty-five to sixty minutes sitting in a pressurised chamber, breathing air with a higher concentration of oxygen than the room has, through a soft mask that is the only thing attached to you. Most people rest, listen to something, or doze, and describe a calm clarity and steadier energy afterwards.

What none of this is

It is not a medical treatment, and nobody here diagnoses anything. It will not make a week with too much in it have less in it, and it is no substitute for sleep, for food, or for advice from your own clinician. If you have a diagnosed condition, follow their advice and tell us about it before you book, so we can be straight with you about whether a session suits.
