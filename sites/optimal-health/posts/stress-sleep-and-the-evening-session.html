Stress, sleep, and the twelve minutes after work

An evening session gives you one dependable thing: half an hour nobody can interrupt. Having it in the diary is why you'll actually take it.

A week that never quite switches off

Most people who book an evening session aren't unwell. They've had a run of weeks with more in them than the weeks were built for: late finishes, a phone that keeps going, sleep that's quietly gone off somewhere around Tuesday. None of that has a name, and none of it needs one before it's worth doing something about.

What happens in the twelve minutes

Twelve minutes on the infrared bed, undressed. The light has to land on skin to get in, so the less you have on the more of you it reaches; underwear is fine, and you're on your own in the room. The bed gives off red and near-infrared light, not hot air, so the warmth reads as deep and even. It's nearer to standing in spring sunshine than to sitting in a sauna. There's little sweating to speak of, no effort, and nothing to recover from afterwards. The whole visit sits inside half an hour.

People book it for the evening because it's easier to wind down after than to work after. A fair number fall asleep on it, which is fine.

The part that isn't the machine

The unglamorous thing is worth saying out loud. A good share of what an evening session gives you is a quiet room, for a set length of time, with your phone somewhere else. We'd rather you left it alone for the twelve minutes, and most people find that the hardest instruction in the building.

Booked time is easier to keep than good intentions. That says nothing about light and everything about diaries, and it's the one thing here we're completely sure of.

What people say about the night after

Two things come up most often: the evening wound down more easily than usual, and they slept better that night. That's what clients tell us, not a promise we can make. Sleep has a great many inputs and a twelve-minute session is one of them.

For a different kind of tired

Where a week has left you slow rather than wound up, people tend to book the chamber instead. That's forty-five to sixty minutes sitting in a pressurised chamber, breathing air with a higher concentration of oxygen than the room has, through a soft mask and nothing else. Most people read, rest, or doze, and describe a calm clarity and steadier energy afterwards.

What this isn't

None of it is a medical treatment, and nobody here diagnoses anything. It won't make a week with too much in it have less in it, and it's no substitute for sleep, for food, or for advice from your own clinician. If you have a diagnosed condition, follow their advice and tell us about it before you book, so we can be straight with you about whether a session suits.
